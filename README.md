## Part 1 findings

The synthetic sample mean is 8.9382 hundred INR, versus the planning assumption of 8.0 hundred INR. The two-sided z-statistic is 2.8930, above the 1.96 critical value, so the null hypothesis is rejected at 5%. A Type I error would mean incorrectly concluding the mean order value differs from INR 800 when it actually equals INR 800. The observed difference is 0.9382 hundred INR, or INR 93.82 per order, potentially relevant to budgeting, but its business impact requires margin and volume information.

The estimated required sample size is 1293 versus 800 available, so the existing sample does not meet the requested INR 50 margin of error at 95% confidence under the sample-SD approximation.

The gradient-descent slope gap is **0.0000** after 500 batch updates, agreeing with the closed-form slope **0.6701**; squared-error loss is convex and the chosen learning rate is stable. Raw-scale residual standard deviations are **2.3155** (lower fitted half) and **8.6891** (upper), a **3.7525** ratio showing heteroscedasticity. After logging both variables, the corresponding residual SDs are **0.5165** and **0.5369**, ratio **1.0396**, much closer to equal spread. The raw-scale and log-scale R² values should not be compared directly because they describe different response scales. For the log outcome, multiple regression adjusted R² **0.7403** exceeds the single-predictor log-log adjusted R² **0.6561**, though adding predictors does not establish causal effects.

## Part 2 findings

All calculations are generated by `part2.py` from the synthetic `data/records.csv`. The class-stratified file-order split has 560 training records (119 positives) and 240 test records (51 positives). Every feature is standardised using training-only statistics, including the binary flags.

**R2.1 — Learning rates.** After 200 batch-gradient updates, the training losses from lowest to highest are: eta 1.0 (0.3703), eta 0.1 (0.3776), eta 0.01 (0.5091). The small learning rate has not converged as far in 200 steps; the largest tested rate converges fastest here, although an excessively large rate could destabilise training.

**R2.2 — Weights and odds.** The three largest absolute logistic weights are X3 (`discount_order_share`, +0.9013), X1 (`customer_tenure_months`, -0.5554), and X2 (`complaint_count_90d`, +0.5505). X3 has the largest odds ratio (2.4629): increasing discount-order share by one training standard deviation multiplies the estimated odds of 90-day churn by about 2.46, holding the other standardised predictors constant. These are associations, not causal effects.

**R2.3 — Thresholds and imbalance.** Predicting no churn for everybody gives 0.7875 accuracy, while logistic regression at tau 0.5 gives 0.8042 accuracy. Accuracy alone hides missed churn cases: at tau 0.5 recall is only 0.4706 (24 of 51). Lowering the threshold to 0.3 raises recall from 0.4706 to 0.7451, but reduces precision from 0.5455 to 0.4634. This is a useful trade-off when misses are expensive.

**R2.4 — KNN comparison.** The five-fold cross-validation selects K=5. On test data KNN has F1 0.3696 and recall 0.3333, below logistic regression at tau 0.3 (F1 0.5714, recall 0.7451). The training-only K-selection avoids choosing a hyperparameter using the test labels.

**R2.5 — Cost-based decision.** I would select the logistic model with tau 0.15 for a controlled retention pilot, not claim production readiness from synthetic data. Training cost is INR 12,150 at the selected threshold. On test rows its cost is INR 6,050, versus INR 11,800 for logistic tau 0.5 and INR 20,400 if nobody is flagged. The threshold is selected on training rows rather than test rows to preserve the test set as an unbiased final evaluation of the chosen policy. Coupon and missed-margin amounts are assumptions supplied in the assignment.

The ROC point table and all eleven KNN cross-validation scores are in `outputs/part2_thresholds.csv` and `outputs/part2_knn_cv.csv`. All reported quantities are computed by the scripts, with rounding only on output.

## Part 3 findings

**R3.1 — K-means elbow.** Standardising the five continuous predictors with sample SD and applying the specified deterministic starting rows gives WCSS values 2262.6753, 1602.8471, 1494.7732, 1323.7157 and 1278.1691 for K=2 through K=6. The four successive WCSS drops are **659.8282, 108.0739, 171.0575 and 45.5466**. The pinned elbow rule selects **K=3**, because the drop from K=3 to K=4 is less than half the preceding drop. The drops are not monotonically decreasing: pinned initial centroids make the solution reproducible but do not guarantee a global optimum. K=3 is used for all subsequent profiling, irrespective of elbow selection.

**R3.2 — Customer profiles and actions.** These are descriptive clusters, not validated causal customer types. **A — established, loyal shoppers** (322 customers; tenure 47.5028 months; discount share 0.2250; loyalty score 62.1584; churn rate 0.0186): offer loyalty recognition and avoid unnecessary retention coupons. **B — high-order-value, complaint-prone shoppers** (255; order-value predictor X4 28.7192 hundreds INR; complaints 6.0706; churn rate 0.2353): prioritise complaint resolution and service recovery. **C — discount-focused, newer shoppers** (223; tenure 14.3892 months; discount share 0.7118; loyalty score 38.5215; churn rate 0.4664): test targeted onboarding and retention offers with cost monitoring. Segment **C** has the highest outcome rate, **0.4664**. The profile's `mean_value` is a separate observed value outcome, not X4.

**R3.3 — Linkage cuts.** For **single** linkage, the largest height gap is 1.4 (between merge heights 1.4 and 2.8), leaving four clusters: **{E1}, {E2,E3,E6}, {E4}, {E5}**. For **complete** linkage, the largest height gap is 5.0 (between 2.9 and 7.9), leaving two clusters: **{E1,E4,E5}** and **{E2,E3,E6}**. Single linkage uses the nearest cross-cluster pair and can connect groups through a chain of near neighbours. Complete linkage uses the farthest cross-cluster pair and delays joining widely separated groups, so the largest-gap cut occurs later here.

**R3.4 — PCA.** The seven steps are: (1) standardise the five columns, giving a mean vector numerically equal to zero; (2) subtract that mean vector; (3) compute sample covariance X-transpose-X divided by 799; (4) use `numpy.linalg.eigh`; (5) sort eigenvalues descending; (6) retain the leading two eigenvectors and fix each sign so its largest-absolute loading is positive; (7) project centred records onto those vectors. PC1 is driven positively by **X1 (+0.5317)** and **X5 (+0.4418)** and negatively by **X3 (-0.5187)** and **X2 (-0.4100)**, with X4 also negative (-0.2916). The first two components explain **75.93%** of variance, while **three** components reach **86.16%**, exceeding 80%. Mean PC1 scores **A=+1.7773**, **B=-0.7831**, **C=-1.6709** place established loyal shoppers on the positive side and discount-focused newer shoppers on the negative side, with B intermediate. The two-component projection is reported for inspection, but three components are needed to reach the 80% target.

All results come from `part3.py` and are recorded in `outputs/part3_results.txt`, `outputs/part3_segments.csv` and `outputs/part3_hierarchical.csv`.

## Part 4 findings

**R4.1 — Decision split and overfitting.** On the 560 training records, split **S4 (X1 ≤ 24 months)** has the largest information gain (**0.1252**) and Gini gain (**0.0606**) among the four candidates. Its yes-side churn rate is **0.4574** and no-side rate **0.0887**, making it informative for identifying newer customers at risk. Growing a tree until every leaf is pure can memorise noise and perform poorly on new customers; a minimum leaf size or maximum depth helps prevent this.

**R4.2 — Voting.** Logistic regression at τ=0.3 has F1 **0.5714** and recall **0.7451**; KNN has **0.3696** and **0.3333**; stump has **0.5532** and **0.7647**; hard vote has **0.5344** and **0.6863**; soft vote has **0.5507** and **0.7451**. Logistic regression has the strongest F1 here, and neither voting combination improves it. For the cost-driven retention policy, I would use the separately selected logistic threshold **0.15**, subject to validation on real data.

**R4.3 — Boosting.** After round 1, the two incorrectly classified points (3 and 7) each have weight **0.2500**, for a combined **0.5000**, compared with their combined initial weight **0.2500**. The second weak rule h2 gets a larger coefficient (**0.8047**) than h1 (**0.5493**) because its weighted error on the reweighted observations is lower (**0.1667** versus **0.2500**).

RNN parameters are reused at each token position, so longer sequences require more computation and memory but not more learned parameters. I would use ReLU in the hidden layers and softmax outputs with categorical cross-entropy for 10 mutually exclusive categories. Very deep plain networks can suffer vanishing gradients, leaving early layers with weak updates. Skip connections provide shorter paths for information and gradients, improving trainability.

## Model-choice memo

**To: Head of Retention Team**

I recommend a controlled pilot of the **from-scratch logistic regression classifier** with the training-selected **0.15 alert threshold**, rather than shipping a more complex ensemble. The 560-row training set contains 119 churn cases, and the 240-row test set contains 51. On test data, logistic regression at threshold 0.3 achieves F1 **0.5714** and recall **0.7451**, exceeding the hard-vote F1 **0.5344** and soft-vote F1 **0.5507**. The decision stump reaches recall **0.7647** but has a lower F1 of **0.5532**.

With the specified cost of INR 50 per false alarm and INR 400 per missed churn, the logistic policy at threshold **0.15** costs **INR 6,050** on test data, compared with **INR 11,800** at threshold 0.5 and **INR 20,400** when no customers are flagged. The 0.15 threshold was chosen on training data, not optimised against test labels.

These results are from synthetic records and one fixed split, so they do not establish actual retention savings or production reliability. Before deployment, validate calibration, stability, and coupon economics on real, consented records and test the intervention experimentally. I would not prioritise the image-classification Model A or text-classification Model B before the structured retention model is validated.

## Limitations

1. All records are synthetic; no actual Flipkart customer data, outcomes, or operational effects are observed.
2. One fixed stratified split and five training folds do not quantify uncertainty under other time periods or populations.
3. Estimated INR costs assume fixed penalties and omit actual intervention effectiveness, targeting capacity and implementation overhead.
4. Predictive associations and unsupervised segment profiles are not causal evidence; segments may change with new customers or initialization.
5. The neural-network section counts theoretical parameters only; no image or text model has been trained or validated.
